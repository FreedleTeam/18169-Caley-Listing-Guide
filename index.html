What might be making buyers cautious?

We’re approaching Thanksgiving, already a period when buyers commonly shift attention toward travel and family. We’ve also just had the midterm elections and a volatile week in the stock market. The week ending November 6 saw the S&P 500 fall about 2.7%, the Nasdaq Composite about 3.4%, and the Nasdaq-100 roughly 4.9%.

Here’s a thought I have, but I want to be clear that it's a theory, not something I can prove with this housing data: a buyer shopping around $750,000 may have some meaningful savings or investments earmarked for a move. A market drop can feel especially uncomfortable, even if they believe their investments will recover. Buyers in other price ranges can certainly be affected too; I wouldn’t assume an individual buyer’s investments based on price alone. My question is whether that short-term uncertainty is giving some move-up buyers another reason to wait.

Whatever the reason, our real estate numbers show a slowdown. We don’t need to prove the stock-market theory to respond intelligently to it.

05 · The decision ahead
My advice

One more weekend. Then consider $725,000.

I still believe $745,000 is a reasonable price for your home in a more typical selling season. But we aren't in a typical season. We’re heading into the holidays, and the evidence says buyers have plenty of options and very little urgency.

To sell now, we may need to make your home feel like a particularly good deal. That isn’t the same as saying the home is worth less in every market; it’s about giving today’s buyer a reason to choose yours now.

Why Lewiston changes the equation

You're getting what I think is a good deal on Lewiston—and an important part of that deal is that the seller accepted a contingent offer. That provides flexibility and a measure of protection if your current home doesn't sell, subject to the actual deadlines and terms in our contract.

That flexibility is unusual and valuable. If Lewiston falls through, I would not count on another seller of a beautiful, desirable home accepting the same contingent terms before the market becomes more favorable to contingent buyers. That timing is my judgment, not a guarantee; it could happen sooner, but I wouldn't want to build our plan around it.

You’re drawn to beautiful homes, and other buyers tend to be drawn to those same properties. Those homes are often the ones where sellers can be choosier. This is why I want us to look at the two transactions together—not just try to squeeze out the last dollar on Caley in isolation.

STEP 01 · THIS WEEKEND

Stay at $745,000

Keep the current price through one more weekend, maintain marketing exposure and open-house opportunities, and watch for real private tours or concrete follow-up—not just clicks.

STEP 02 · IF ACTIVITY STAYS FLAT

Adjust to $725,000

If meaningful buyer activity hasn't appeared after the weekend, my advice is a $20,000 price reduction (about 2.7%). We would be intentionally competing for the limited buyers who are ready to act.

I prepared you for the possibility of $725,000 before we launched. I think it’s worth giving our current price one more weekend—but protecting a good purchase on Lewiston is a meaningful part of the equation.

The tradeoff: The proposed adjustment is $20,000 off asking price, not a promise of a sale at $725,000. We would still evaluate offers based on price, contingencies, concessions, timing, and how reliably they let you move forward on Lewiston.
Bottom line

Our job is to make the next move possible.

We’ve done the right things to launch the listing, and the exposure is encouraging. Now we have to pay attention to what buyers are actually doing. I want to give it one more opportunity at $745,000, then be prepared to act decisively if the market stays this quiet.

I’ll continue watching the competing homes and any fresh pending activity as we approach that decision.
